# ITEM 1 — Slide 17: Abortion Scope of Practice for CNMs

**Complete Rebuild Required**

> **UPDATED SLIDE DATA:** The Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization decision (June 24, 2022) eliminated the federal constitutional right to abortion and returned all regulatory authority to individual states. As of early 2026, the abortion landscape for CNMs breaks down as follows: (1) Near-total bans in approximately 12–13 states (Alabama, Arkansas, Idaho, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, and West Virginia, with North Dakota and Missouri in active litigation). CNMs cannot perform medication or procedural abortions in these states. Criminal penalties attach to providers. (2) Six-week gestational bans in Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Ohio, South Carolina, and North Carolina (12 weeks). Georgia's LIFE Act (HB 481, 2019, effective post-Dobbs 2022) prohibits abortion at approximately 6 weeks from last menstrual period with narrow exceptions. CNMs in these states have severely curtailed or no practice authority for abortion care. (3) 15–22 week bans in a smaller number of states including portions of Arizona, Indiana, Nebraska, and Utah. (4) Approximately 25 states and D.C. retain or have expanded abortion access — California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, and Wisconsin. CNMs may perform medication and procedural abortions where state law, scope of practice, and institutional credentialing permit. The slide should reference the Center for Reproductive Rights After Roe Fell tracker (updated February 2026) for current state-by-state categorizations.
>
> ***WHY CHANGE:** The original Slide 17 used 2018 AP Toolkit data showing pre-Dobbs abortion scope permissions. That legal reality ceased to exist on June 24, 2022. Presenting it as current is not merely inaccurate — it could lead a CNM or student to make career decisions based on practice rights that no longer exist, or worse, unknowingly expose themselves to criminal liability in a ban state. The Milbank Memorial Fund (October 2025) documented that maternal mortality rose 56% in states with abortion bans post-Dobbs while declining 21% in permissive states — directly connecting this policy slide to the maternal mortality outcome data in the same deck. Slide 26 (Medicaid Coverage of Abortions) requires the same level of scrutiny and complete rebuild.*

# ITEM 3 — Georgia: Certificate of Need, Letter of Intent, and CNM Practice Rights

**Significant 2024 Legislative Changes; Missing from Presentation**

> **UPDATED SLIDE DATA:** Georgia retains its full Certificate of Need (CON) program as of 2026. Under O.C.G.A. § 31-6 et seq., any person proposing a new institutional health service or health care facility — including a freestanding birth center — must file a mandatory Letter of Intent (LOI) and obtain a CON from the Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH) Office of Health Planning. House Bill 1339 (signed by Governor Kemp, April 19, 2024; effective July 1, 2024) revised the CON framework but did not eliminate it. Key HB 1339 changes: (1) The LOI must now be filed 25 days prior to the CON Application (previously 30 days). (2) New CON exemptions were created for single-specialty ambulatory surgical centers meeting capital thresholds. (3) Birth centers and new obstetric services are NOT exempt and still require full CON review. The DCH has a 120-day review period beginning upon application submission. Additional CON legislative changes were expected in 2025 (DCH was directed to submit reform proposals to the legislature by December 1, 2024). Georgia CNMs require a physician collaborative agreement and do not have full practice authority. As of 2024, approximately 380 CNMs/CMs practice in Georgia. Georgia has two ACME-accredited programs: Emory University and Georgia College and State University.
>
> ***WHY CHANGE:** The original 2021 presentation does not address CON laws as a birth center access barrier, even though they are among the most consequential facility-level regulatory constraints for midwifery practice expansion. For anyone considering opening a birth center in Georgia, the LOI/CON process is a significant regulatory and financial hurdle. HB 1339 updated but did not eliminate this barrier. The Georgia CON framework should be represented both on the regulatory structure slide and on any birth center slide as a structural access barrier that does not exist in states that have eliminated CON laws.*

# ITEM 9 — New Slide Needed: Telehealth and CNM Practice

**Entirely Absent from 2021 Deck**

> **UPDATED SLIDE DATA:** A new slide on telehealth should be added to the Laws and Regulations section. Content: (1) Federal framework: COVID-19 PHE-era telehealth flexibilities substantially expanded CNM remote care capabilities. Many were extended through the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 and subsequent legislation, with some proposed for permanent inclusion in the 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule. Telehealth originating site restrictions (requiring rural/HPSA patient location) were waived during the PHE; some of these waivers expired at end of 2025 for Medicare while states have varied Medicaid telehealth policies. (2) State variation: CNM telehealth practice varies by state across four dimensions — which services are covered and reimbursed; whether out-of-state telehealth requires licensure in the patient's state; whether audio-only visits are reimbursed comparably to video; and whether prescribing via telehealth (including contraception and postpartum medications) is permitted. (3) Key obstetric applications: Remote fetal monitoring, postpartum depression screening and follow-up, lactation consulting, blood pressure monitoring for hypertensive pregnancies, medication abortion (where CNMs can provide it), and prenatal education. Remote blood pressure monitoring postpartum has been specifically documented to reduce maternal mortality from hypertensive crises.
>
> ***WHY CHANGE:** The total absence of telehealth from a practice environment presentation in 2026 is a significant gap. Telehealth policy is now a permanent and substantial dimension of the CNM practice environment, directly affecting scope of practice, geographic reach, revenue generation, and patient access. For CNMs in maternity care deserts or rural areas, telehealth capability is now a core practice competency and business requirement — as important as understanding CON laws, collaborative agreements, or Medicaid reimbursement rates.*
